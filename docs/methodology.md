# Methodology (plain English)

## Signal time versus execution time
A daily strategy can only make a decision after the market closes, because only then is
the closing price known. The earliest it can act is the next morning's open. If a
backtest buys at the same close that produced the signal, it trades on a price it could
not have acted on, and results look better than reality. Here every order is created
after a close and filled at the next tradable open (`docs/execution_and_accounting.md`).

## Why daily bars hide the intraday path
A daily bar gives four prices and a volume. It does not say whether the high came before
the low, or where the price was when an order would have reached the market. So the
engine fills only at the open, does not simulate stops or limits inside the bar, and
estimates impact from *previous* days' volume and volatility.

## How cash and holdings determine equity
Equity = cash + value of every position at the latest price (short positions count as
negative). Buying 100 shares at 50 moves 5,000 from cash into the position. Equity
changes only through price moves, costs and interest. The engine checks this identity
at every close.

## How transaction costs affect returns
Every trade pays half the bid-ask spread, some slippage, market impact that grows with
the square root of order size relative to average volume, and commission. These are
small per trade but add up with turnover: on the synthetic SMA sample, flat costs of
50 bps one-way cut Sharpe by more than half (see README). A strategy that only works at
zero cost is not usable.

## Sharpe versus Sortino
Sharpe divides the average excess return by the standard deviation of excess returns,
so upside and downside swings are penalised equally. Sortino divides by downside
deviation only (the root-mean-square of returns below the risk-free target). Sortino
matters for skewed returns. A strategy with rare large gains and small losses looks
better on Sortino than on Sharpe.

## Training, validation and the final hold-out
* **Training window**: where parameters are chosen.
* **Validation / test window**: the following year, traded with the chosen parameters
  frozen. In walk-forward analysis this repeats as the windows roll forward, and the
  test years are stitched into one out-of-sample record.
* **Final hold-out**: the last three years (2022–2024 here), set aside before any
  experiment. It is removed from the data that searches can see, and can only be
  evaluated with an explicit `--confirm` command that is logged.

## Walk-forward optimisation
1. Split history into rolling folds: 3 years of training, then 1 year of testing.
2. In each training window, run every parameter set and log it.
3. Pick the best by training Sharpe (ties broken deterministically).
4. Trade the next year with that choice.
5. Run all test years as **one continuous portfolio**, so moving from one fold's
   parameters to the next creates real trades and costs. There are no free resets.

## Why repeated searches inflate performance
If you try many random strategies with no real edge, the best one will still look good
by luck. The more you try, the better the best looks. Reporting only the winner hides
this.

## What the Deflated Sharpe Ratio does and does not establish
DSR asks how likely it is that the true Sharpe of the chosen strategy exceeds the Sharpe
you would expect from the *best of N skill-less trials*, after allowing for short
samples and fat tails. It needs an honest N, which is why every trial is logged,
including invalid ones. It is **not** the probability of future profit. It does not
prove alpha, and it cannot account for ideas tested outside the framework.

## Why survivorship bias may remain
If the data only contain companies that still exist today, every company that went
bankrupt is missing, and the historical universe looks healthier than it was. The
membership interface prevents trading assets before they are known to be members. It
cannot restore data that were never in the file. The optional market sample (ten US
sector ETFs chosen today) is not point-in-time.

## Why backtest success does not guarantee future profits
A backtest reports one path of history under modelling assumptions about costs,
liquidity and fills. Markets change, edges decay once found, and estimates from 15–20
years of daily data carry wide uncertainty (see the bootstrap confidence intervals). The
synthetic data here are generated by a model chosen by a person, so performance on them
says nothing about real markets.
